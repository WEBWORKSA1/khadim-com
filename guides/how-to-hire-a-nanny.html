How to hire a nanny you can trust

Choosing someone to care for your child is the biggest hire most families ever make. This step-by-step guide covers everything from the job description to the first week.

By the Khadim editorial team. Last checked 29 October 2026. 3 min read.

Key facts

  • Start looking six to eight weeks before you need care; good nannies are often booked ahead.
  • Always do a paid trial — at least one full day, ideally a week.
  • Check at least two references from parents, not only agencies.
  • Safe-sleep knowledge and basic infant first aid matter more than any certificate.
  • Put pay, hours, duties and notice in a written agreement before day one.

1. Write down what you need

Before you post an ad or call an agency, write a one-page job description. It forces you to decide, and it lets candidates judge if they're a fit.

  • Children's ages and anything specific — allergies, a newborn, special needs.
  • Hours and days, including early starts, late finishes and travel.
  • Duties: childcare only, or also the children's laundry, meals and school runs.
  • Live-in or live-out.
  • Languages you'd like spoken with the children.
  • Pay range — check our pay planner so it's realistic.

2. Where to look

  • Personal referrals from parents at school, nursery or your building.
  • Licensed nanny agencies, which pre-screen and often offer a replacement period.
  • Nanny platforms, useful for comparing many profiles; verify what checks they actually do.
  • Khadim: send a request and get options in 24 hours.

3. Screen by phone first

A 15-minute call saves hours. Confirm availability, pay expectations and experience with children of your children's ages. Ask why they're leaving their current job. Listen for warmth and clarity as much as for answers.

4. The interview

Meet at home so they see the space and you see how they react to it. Let your child be around for part of it. Ask questions that need examples, not yes-or-no answers:

  • "Tell me about a typical day with the last family you worked for."
  • "What would you do if my toddler had a tantrum in a shop?"
  • "How do you put a baby down to sleep?" (Listen for: on their back, on a firm, flat surface, nothing loose in the cot.)
  • "What would you do if my child had a high fever and you couldn't reach me?"
  • "How do you handle screen time, snacks and discipline?"

Our interview guide has 60 questions with a printable scorecard.

5. The paid trial

Nothing replaces watching someone care for your child. Arrange a paid trial day or week while you're at home. Notice how they engage, how your child responds, how they handle routines and whether they follow your instructions — and whether they speak up when they disagree. Pay for every trial day.

6. References and checks

Call at least two previous families. Ask:

  • How long did they work for you, and what were their duties?
  • How did your children feel about them?
  • Was there ever a time you were worried? What happened?
  • Why did they leave, and would you hire them again?

Check identity and, where your country allows it, a police or background check — with the candidate's consent. See our safety and checks guide.

7. Training that matters

Paediatric first aid and CPR training is worth paying for if your nanny doesn't have it. At minimum, watch these together before the first day: baby choking first aid and safe sleep videos. Agree a written emergency plan with your numbers, your paediatrician, the nearest hospital and any allergies.

8. Pay, contract and the first week

Agree pay, hours, overtime, a weekly day off, paid leave and notice in writing. Use our contract builder. In the first week, work alongside your nanny for a day or two, share the routines in writing, and set a short weekly check-in so small issues never become big ones.

Red flags

  • Reluctance to do a trial or give references.
  • Vague or changing stories about previous jobs.
  • Dismissive answers about safe sleep or first aid.
  • Pressure to pay large amounts in advance.

Can I use a camera?

Many families use cameras in shared rooms. If you do, tell your nanny in writing, never place cameras in a bathroom or their private room, and check local privacy law. Trust built through openness lasts longer than trust built through surveillance.
