Domestic workers' rights: a fair employer's guide

What the law requires in six countries, what the international standard says, and the everyday practices that make a home a good place to work.

By the Khadim editorial team. Last checked 29 October 2026. 3 min read.

Key facts

  • About 75.6 million people worldwide are domestic workers — 4.5% of all workers — and 81% work informally (ILO, 2021).
  • The ILO Domestic Workers Convention, 2011 (No. 189) has been ratified by 40 countries as of June 2026.
  • C189 calls for written terms, weekly rest of at least 24 consecutive hours, minimum wage coverage, monthly pay, and workers keeping their own documents.
  • India, the Gulf states, Singapore and Hong Kong have not ratified C189, but each has its own rules.

What the international standard says

The ILO Domestic Workers Convention (C189), adopted in 2011, recognises domestic work as work and sets minimum protections:

  • Clear terms of employment, in writing where possible (Article 7).
  • Free choice about living in, and workers keep their travel and identity documents (Article 9).
  • Equal treatment with other workers on hours, overtime and rest, and weekly rest of at least 24 consecutive hours (Article 10).
  • Minimum wage coverage where minimum wages exist (Article 11), and pay at least monthly (Article 12).
  • Safe and healthy working conditions and social security (Articles 13 and 14).
  • Regulation of private employment agencies, and no recruitment fees deducted from wages (Article 15).

Documents belong to the worker

Keeping a worker's passport "for safekeeping" is banned in the UAE and Saudi Arabia and against international standards. It traps workers and destroys trust. If a worker wants you to store their documents, give them access whenever they ask and put that agreement in writing.

Recruitment fees: who pays

In the UAE and Saudi Arabia, employers pay recruitment and government costs; charging workers is prohibited. Hong Kong caps what agencies can charge helpers at 10% of the first month's wages. Many workers still arrive in debt to brokers in their home countries. Ask your agency directly whether the worker paid anything, and choose agencies that commit to zero fees.

Everyday fairness

  • Pay on time, in full, with a record.
  • Protect rest — no "quick tasks" on the day off.
  • A private, safe room, proper food and clean water.
  • Let workers keep their phones and contact family.
  • Respect, never abuse — including in front of children and guests.
  • Medical care when they're ill.
  • A fair hearing when things go wrong, and an honest reference when they leave.

When something goes wrong

Most disputes start small. Talk early, in private, with an interpreter if needed. If you can't resolve it, the labour ministry in your country — MOHRE in the UAE, HRSD and Musaned in Saudi Arabia, the Ministry of Labour in Qatar, the Ministry of Manpower in Singapore, the Labour Department in Hong Kong — handles domestic worker disputes. Workers can also contact their embassy.
