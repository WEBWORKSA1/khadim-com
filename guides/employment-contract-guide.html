What to put in a domestic worker contract

A written agreement is the cheapest insurance a household can buy. Here's what each clause should say, why it matters, and sample wording you can adapt.

By the Khadim editorial team. Last checked 29 October 2026. 3 min read.

Key facts

  • ILO Convention 189 says domestic workers should be told their terms of employment in writing, in a way they understand.
  • Some places require an official contract: Hong Kong's Standard Employment Contract, Saudi Arabia's Musaned contract, and UAE contracts through MOHRE-licensed offices.
  • Where the law gives a worker more than your contract, the law wins.
  • Give the worker their own signed copy, in a language they read.

1. The parties and the start date

Full names of the employer and worker, the home address where the work happens, and the start date. If the worker is employed by an agency and placed with you, say so — it changes who is responsible for pay.

2. Role and duties

List the main duties. Be specific enough that both of you would agree on whether something is "in the job". Add a line that new duties need agreement and may change pay.

Sample: "Main duties are cleaning the home, laundry and ironing, and preparing breakfast and dinner for four people. Other duties need the worker's agreement and may change the pay."

3. Pay

State the monthly amount, the pay date, the method (bank transfer, UPI, WPS, Musaned wallet), and how overtime or extra days are paid. List any allowances — food, phone, travel — separately so they can't be confused with salary. Say that deductions are only made where the law allows and the worker agrees in writing.

4. Working hours and rest

Normal daily hours, breaks, and the weekly day off. For live-in workers, say when the working day ends and protect unbroken sleep. The UAE requires at least 12 hours' daily rest including 8 consecutive hours; Saudi Arabia 8 continuous hours; Qatar caps work at 10 hours a day. The ILO standard for weekly rest is at least 24 consecutive hours.

5. Leave and holidays

Annual paid leave, public holidays and paid sick leave. Agree how leave is requested and how much notice each side gives. In the UAE, the minimum is 30 days' paid annual leave; in Qatar three weeks; in Saudi Arabia a month after two years.

6. Accommodation and food (live-in)

A private room with a lock, bedding, sanitation, and either food or a food allowance. In Hong Kong the minimum food allowance is HK$1,236 a month if food isn't provided.

7. Documents, phone and freedom of movement

The worker keeps their own passport, ID and phone. On their day off they go where they choose. This clause matters because confiscating documents is common, illegal in many countries, and the fastest way to lose a good worker's trust.

8. Trial period and notice

A trial period of two weeks to a month for local hires; six months is set by law in the UAE. After it, either side gives the agreed notice — 30 days is common — or pays in lieu. Say what's paid on the last day: wages, unused leave, any bonus and end-of-service benefit due.

9. End-of-service benefits

Where the law sets a gratuity — such as Qatar's three weeks' wage per year, or Saudi Arabia's one month after four years — write it in. In the UAE, where the position is unsettled, we recommend agreeing at least 14 days' basic wage per year of service.

10. Dignity and disputes

A short line that the home is a harassment-free workplace, and a simple way to raise problems — a weekly check-in, or a named relative to speak to. Name the authority to contact if a dispute can't be solved.

Signing

  • Read it together, in a language the worker understands, and translate if needed.
  • Both sign and date every page.
  • Each keeps a copy. Update it when pay or duties change.
