How to hire a maid or domestic helper in India

A practical guide for families in Indian cities — what to pay in 2026, where to find reliable help, how verification works, and the small things that make good people stay.

By the Khadim editorial team. Last checked 29 October 2026. 5 min read.

Key facts

  • India has no single national law for domestic work. The four Labour Codes, in force since 21 November 2025, cover domestic workers mainly through the Code on Social Security.
  • Several states — including Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Rajasthan — include domestic work in their minimum wage schedules.
  • A full-time housekeeper in a metro city typically earns ₹12,000–₹20,000 a month in 2026; cooks, nannies and drivers earn more.
  • Police verification of domestic help is offered by city police forces and is usually voluntary, but strongly recommended.
  • Domestic workers can register on the government's e-Shram portal to access social security schemes.

1. Decide exactly what you need

Most hiring problems in Indian homes start with a vague brief. Write down the tasks, the hours and the days before you speak to anyone. Then choose one of four common arrangements:

  • Part-time, per task. One person comes daily for sweeping and mopping, another for utensils, a cook for meals. Pay is usually per task per month. Flexible and affordable, but you manage several people.
  • Full-time, live-out. One helper for eight to ten hours a day, six days a week, covering most of the home. Easier to manage; costs more.
  • Live-in. The helper stays with you, which suits elder care and families with young children. It brings extra duties for you: a private room, food, rest hours and a weekly day off.
  • Short-term or specialist. Newborn care (japa) for one to three months, or cover when your regular help is on leave.

2. What to pay in 2026

Pay depends on your city, the neighbourhood, the tasks and the worker's experience. These are typical monthly ranges in metro cities such as Mumbai, Delhi NCR, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai and Pune. Expect roughly 20–40% less in tier-2 cities and towns.

If your state has a minimum wage for domestic workers, that is the floor — but in big cities the market rate is usually well above it. Offering a little above the going rate is the single most effective way to attract and keep reliable people. Use the pay planner for your exact duties and hours.

3. Where to find good help

  • Referrals. Neighbours, your building's watchman, your resident welfare association and existing helpers are still the most common route, and references come built in.
  • Placement agencies. They save time and often offer a replacement period. Fees are commonly around one month's salary. Ask whether the agency is registered with your state labour department where registration is required, and make sure the worker isn't being charged too.
  • Online platforms. Useful for comparing profiles, especially for cooks and nannies. Check what verification the platform actually does.

4. Interview and a paid trial

Meet in person, with someone who speaks the worker's language if needed. Ask about previous homes, what they enjoyed and what went wrong, how they handle breakages or illness, and what they expect in pay and days off. Our 60 interview questions include role-specific sets.

Then offer a paid trial day or week. Pay for every trial day — it shows respect and gets you a truer picture.

5. Verification: ID, references and police

Identity. Ask to see an original ID and keep a copy only of what you need. If you use Aadhaar, ask for a masked copy that hides most of the number. Store copies securely and return or delete them when the job ends.

References. Call at least one previous employer. Ask how long the person worked there, why they left, and whether they would hire them again.

Police verification. Most city police forces run a domestic help verification service. Delhi Police, for example, offers registration of domestic help through its online citizen services portal; in other cities you can apply online or at the local police station with the worker's photo, ID and address. It is usually voluntary, but it protects both of you. Explain to the worker why you're doing it and ask for their consent.

6. Put it in writing

A one-page agreement prevents most disputes. Include duties, hours, the weekly day off, pay and pay date, leave, notice period, and anything extra such as a phone allowance or meals. Our contract builder creates one in five minutes; read it together and both sign.

7. Pay, leave and the festival bonus

  • Pay on a fixed day, ideally by bank transfer or UPI, so both of you have a record.
  • A weekly day off — and if you ask them to work it, pay extra or give another day.
  • Paid leave. Agree an annual allowance — 15 to 30 days is common for full-time help — plus paid sick days.
  • Festival bonus. About one month's pay once a year, at Diwali, Eid, Christmas or the festival that matters to them, is customary across India.
  • Annual raise. A raise each year in line with prices keeps good people from drifting away.

8. Help them register for social security

The government's e-Shram portal registers unorganised workers, including domestic workers. By March 2023, about 2.79 crore domestic and household workers had registered. Registration opens access to schemes such as life and accident insurance (PMJJBY and PMSBY), a pension scheme (PM-SYM) and health cover (AB-PMJAY), subject to each scheme's eligibility rules. Some states, such as Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu, also run welfare boards for domestic workers. Helping your worker register costs you nothing and is one of the most valuable things you can do.

9. Small things that make good people stay

  • Clean drinking water, a place to rest, and use of a toilet.
  • Meals or tea if they work long hours.
  • Respect in front of guests and children.
  • Advance notice of changes to hours or duties.
  • An honest reference when they leave.

10. If it doesn't work out

Give the notice you agreed, or pay in lieu. Settle all pay, leave and any bonus due on the last day. Never withhold pay or documents. A calm, fair ending protects your reputation in the neighbourhood — and good helpers talk to each other.
